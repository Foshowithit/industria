# BUILD-INTENT ADDENDUM 1 — what the planning seat missed

Append to `BUILD-INTENT.md`. These are additional **brief requirements for the first hour** that the
planning seat did not carry into the first brief. They are not optional garnish; §7, §40, §55 and §56
are load-bearing for exactly the R2 scope being built.

---

## A. §7 — THE FIRST CHOICE IS A JOB BOARD, NOT A CHARACTER CLASS

Verbatim from the brief:

> The first major player choice should not be a traditional character class.
> It should be: **"What work can I get?"**
> … There should be no single correct career.

The brief gives the entry list and even the rates. Reproduce this shape — the numbers below are the
brief's own and should be used, not invented:

| role | rate | requirement |
|---|---|---|
| CNC Operator I | $23.50/hr | 2nd shift, training available |
| Machine Shop Helper | $20/hr | no experience required |
| Quality Technician Trainee | $24/hr | blueprint experience preferred |
| Maintenance Apprentice | $26/hr | mechanical aptitude required |
| Welder/Fabricator Trainee | $22/hr | — |
| Production Technician | $21/hr | — |
| Toolroom Apprentice | $25/hr | — |
| Automation Technician I | $28/hr | electrical experience preferred |

**Why this matters mechanically:** the player should arrive at the parallels-and-calipers opening
*via their own choice*, having weighed pay against what they think they can get. The rate is then
real to them — it is *their* rate — which is what makes the first payslip, and later the scrap
charge, land. A player who picked "Machine Shop Helper, $20, no experience required" understands
the pressure of the parallels task differently from one who bluffed into "Automation Technician I".

Note the honest design consequence: **several of these paths do not lead to a lathe at all.** The
brief explicitly says there is no single correct career. If only one board entry is implemented now,
the board must still show the others as real choices with real consequences, not as dead buttons.
★ If they cannot be built this run, show the board and make the others honest — "you took the
toolroom apprenticeship" must enter the same world, not a placeholder screen.

## B. §40 — HUMAN FACTORS, AND THE ONE LINE THAT CONSTRAINS ALL NPC WRITING

> Real manufacturing includes: fatigue · communication problems · **poor instructions** · confidence ·
> mentorship · interpersonal conflict · pride · craftsmanship · shortcuts · pressure
>
> Handle this carefully and believably. **Avoid melodramatic NPC writing.**
> **A good line from a senior machinist may teach more than a cutscene.**

Two hard consequences for Earl and everyone else:

1. **No melodrama.** No speeches, no inspirational monologues, no villain supervisor. Earl is tired
   at 5:55 AM. He is good at his job and he is not there to develop your character.
2. **POOR INSTRUCTIONS ARE A DESIGNED FEATURE, NOT A BUG.** *"Clean that vise and bring me the
   parallels"* is deliberately underspecified — the player is supposed to not know what parallels
   are, and to feel the gap between what was said and what was meant. **Do not "improve" this line by
   specifying it.** The confusion is the mechanic. What must be fair is that the world contains
   enough to resolve the confusion: the parallels can be found, and Earl will answer if asked
   properly. Unfair confusion is a bug; resolvable confusion is the game.

## C. §55 — MENTORS HAVE METHODS, BIASES AND BLIND SPOTS

> A 30-year machinist may teach phenomenal practical knowledge while disliking new CAM strategies.
> A young engineer may know simulation but lack shop intuition. The player learns to synthesize
> perspectives.

Earl must therefore be **right and also wrong about things** — his practical knowledge is
excellent, his opinions are not automatically correct, and some of what he says should later be
shown to be shop lore rather than physics. This is also the cleanest way to teach the over-dial walk
honestly: Earl says the thing every real machinist says, the part then behaves the way the kernel
says it behaves, and the player discovers that both were partly right. **The kernel is the authority;
Earl is a witness.** Do not make Earl a help menu.

## D. §56 — DOCUMENTATION IS PART OF THE WORLD AND PART OF THE DIFFICULTY

> Players encounter: setup sheets · drawings · work instructions · SOPs · inspection plans ·
> maintenance logs · BOMs · routers · certs · calibration records.
> **Good documentation improves organizations. Bad documentation produces mistakes.**

These are diegetic objects on the floor (§48), not menu screens. The traveler taped to the machine,
the drawing in a plastic sleeve, the calibration sticker on the gauge, the SOP binder nobody has
opened since 2019. And the difficulty is honest: **the drawing on the floor may be the wrong
revision**, and a part made to the old revision is scrap. That is a real shop failure and it teaches
revision control by consequence — the §2 loop, not a lesson.
